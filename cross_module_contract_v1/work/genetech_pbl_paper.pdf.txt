1 Introduction
Ageing is accompanied by progressive, characteristic changes in gene expression that degrade cellu-
lar function. A central observation of the past two decades is that these changes are not necessarily
permanent: transient expression of the Yamanaka factors (Sox2,Oct4,Klf4,Myc; hereafter SOKM),
the same factors that convert somatic cells into induced pluripotent stem cells, can restore youthful
expression patterns in aged cells without driving them to full pluripotency. This process is termed
partial, or transient, reprogramming.
The approach carries an intrinsic tension. The process that restores youthful expression also
suppresses the programmes that define somatic cell identity. Pushed too far, cells lose identity, with
associated risks of senescence, uncontrolled proliferation, and tumour formation. Any therapeutic
use of partial reprogramming therefore depends on identifying conditions that rejuvenate without
crossing into unsafe cell states.
Recent single-cell work has made this tension tractable. Roux et al. [1] mapped the identity
trajectory induced by partial reprogramming across several murine cell types and screened subsets
of the Yamanaka factors. They reported that partial reprogramming restored youthful expression in
mesenchymal and adipogenic stem cells while transiently suppressing somatic identity programmes,
and, critically, that rejuvenation and identity suppression were not tightly coupled: different factor
combinations produced different balances of the two effects.
That decoupling is the premise of this project. If rejuvenation and risk were perfectly correlated,
no safe reprogramming condition could exist. Because they are separable, a safe operating window
plausibly exists, and the problem becomes one of search. The search space is large: the four
Yamanaka factors alone yield fifteen non-empty subsets, and the full space extends across dose,
duration, delivery timing, additional transcription factors, and small-molecule alternatives. Gene
regulatory networks make the effects of such interventions difficult to predict from first principles,
and exhaustive experimental screening is infeasible.
This motivates a computational framework with two capabilities: (i) to quantify how young,
and how risky, a given cell state is, and (ii) to predict the effect of an intervention that has not been
experimentally tested. We decompose the problem into three components. AYouth Scoremeasures
how closely a cell’s expression profile resembles that of a young cell. ARisk Scoremeasures unsafe
cell states along three axes: dedifferentiation and identity loss, inflammation and senescence, and

This report presents the perturbation-prediction arm in full. Its intended role was to rank
candidate transcription factors by their predicted effect on the youth axis. We show that, on the
cohort available, this goal is not attainable for a specific and generalisable reason: the aging axis a
foundation model recovers in this data is confounded with transcript complexity, and that confound
is not separable from age with the data at hand. We report this as a characterised negative result,
and we show that the underlying confound replicates across two independent assays, which reframes
it from a property of one dataset into a constraint on the modelling strategy itself.
2 Methods: perturbation prediction
[integration note: This becomes a subsection of the shared Methods section on merge.]
2.1 Model and rationale
We used Geneformer [2], a transformer pretrained on approximately 30 million human single cells,
in a zero-shot setting: the model was not fine-tuned on our cohort. This choice was deliberate.
A gene-token transformer trained from scratch on this project’s data underperformed a simple
gene-signature classifier, consistent with model capacity exceeding the information available in a
cohort of roughly fifteen donors. Zero-shot inference fits no parameters to our donors and therefore
avoids that failure mode. We used theGeneformer-V1-10Mcheckpoint (six layers, input size 2048,
vocabulary 25 426) with its matchedgc30Mtoken dictionary.
2.2 Data and gene mapping
The analysis used the Tabula Muris Senis FACS limb-muscle mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) cohort
in raw counts. After excluding 120 cells annotated as diaphragm rather than limb muscle (Sec-
tion 3.3), 815 cells remained: 264 young (3 months, 6 donors) and 551 old (18 and 24 months,
8 donors). Because Geneformer is human-pretrained, mouse genes were mapped to human or-
thologs via Ensembl BioMart, retaining one-to-one orthologs only: 14 314 of 22 966 genes (62.3 %),
corresponding to 68.9 % of total count mass.
2.3 In-silico perturbation and the goal-state axis
Perturbations were performed with Geneformer’sInSilicoPerturber(perturb type = overexpress,
emb mode = cell). Overexpression is represented by promoting the target gene’s token to the top
of a cell’s rank-value encoding; a gene need not be expressed in the cell to be a valid target. The
goal state was defined by two centroids in embedding space, the mean embeddings of old and
young cells. The reported metric,Shift to goal end, is the perturbed cell’s cosine similarity to
the young centroid minus that of the unperturbed cell, so that positive values denote movement
toward the young state. The sign convention was verified against the model source rather than
assumed. All runs used a fixed seed.

2.4 Axis validation
Because the perturbation metric is measured entirely along the old–young centroid axis, we tested
whether that axis separates young from aged cells before interpreting any perturbation value.
We extracted per-cell embeddings for all 815 cells and evaluated separation at the donor level,
the appropriate unit of replication, using leave-one-donor-out nearest-centroid classification with a
1000-draw donor-label permutation control. We then assessed technical confounding by correlating
each cell’s position along the old–young axis with its total counts and its detected-gene count,
and tested whether separation survived (i) regressing detected-gene count out of each embedding
dimension and (ii) restricting to a matched detected-gene-count band. Finally, we tested whether
detected-gene count itself differs by age at the donor level, and repeated the same test on the
independent Tabula Muris Senis Droplet limb-MSC cohort.
3 Results: perturbation prediction
[integration note: This becomes a subsection of the shared Results section on merge.]
3.1 Preliminary perturbation output
The pipeline ran end to end at approximately 30 s per gene. Seven candidate factors were perturbed
individually (Table 1). The values are small in absolute terms and, for every factor, the per-cell
standard deviation exceeds the mean shift by a factor of roughly 15 to 25. The four SOKM
factors run as a single combined condition gave a positive shift (+0.000 166) while three of the
four individual components were negative, an internal inconsistency. We therefore treat Table 1 as
pipeline output rather than as a result, pending validation of the axis along which it is measured.
3.2 The goal-state axis is confounded with transcript complexity
At the donor level the old–young axis separated the two groups only weakly: leave-one-donor-out
accuracy was 11 of 14, permutationp= 0.050, exactly at the conventional threshold. All three
misclassified donors were young mice assigned to the old group, and each sat near the old centroid,
indicating that the axis succeeds for some young donors and fails for others rather than separating
the groups uniformly.
The cause is technical. Each cell’s position along the axis correlated with its detected-gene count
at Pearsonr= 0.69 (p≈10 −116), while its correlation with total counts was negligible (r=−0.02).

3.3 Age and transcript complexity are not separable in this cohort
Separation collapsed under correction because detected-gene count is itself confounded with age.
Old donors averaged 1768 detected genes and young donors 2734 (Welchp= 0.009; Mann–Whitney
p= 0.020), and the difference persisted within the matched band (2215 vs 2571;p= 0.028). Age
and transcript complexity thus cannot be disentangled with the data available: correcting for one
removes much of the other.
We emphasise that lower detected-gene count in aged cells is not necessarily a pure technical
artifact; a genuine decline in transcriptional output with age would produce the same signal. The
finding is therefore that age and detected-gene count are inseparable in this cohort, not that the axis
is purely artifactual. Either way, perturbation shifts measured along this axis cannot be interpreted
as movement along a biological aging axis.
3.4 The confound replicates across assays
To test whether this is specific to the FACS cohort, we repeated the donor-level test on the indepen-
dent Tabula Muris Senis Droplet limb-MSC cohort, which does not share the FACS preprocessing
pipeline. The direction replicated: old cells averaged 1766 detected genes and young cells 2023
(Welchp= 0.0007; Mann–Whitneyp= 0.03). Because the two assays do not share artifact-
generating steps, their agreement argues that the age–complexity association is a recurring feature
of aged MSC data rather than an artifact of one protocol. Two caveats temper the Droplet result:
it contains only two young donors, both female, so it corroborates rather than independently es-
tablishes the effect and cannot separate age from sex; and the effect size differs markedly between
assays (a gap of∼970 detected genes in FACS versus∼260 in Droplet), consistent with a mixture
of genuine biology and assay-dependent technical behaviour.
Discussion contribution
[integration note: These paragraphs feed the shared Discussion; they are not a standalone section.]
The perturbation arm returned a negative but diagnostic result. On the available cohort,
a human-pretrained foundation model recovers an aging axis that is substantially a transcript-
complexity axis, and because complexity is confounded with age at the donor level, the confound
cannot be removed with these data. The same age–complexity association appears in a second
assay, which converges with an independent observation from the Youth Score analysis, where
a purely technical principal component separated age groups at AUC 0.854.[integration note:

This has a concrete implication for the project’s next stage. Because the age–complexity link
is at least partly intrinsic and replicates across assays, simply assembling a larger multi-dataset
cohort will not, on its own, remove it; a harmonised cohort would need to model or condition on
transcript complexity explicitly rather than balancing donor counts alone. The negative result is,
in this sense, a positive constraint on how the modelling should proceed.
